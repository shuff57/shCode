# Module 2.6: Chapter 2 Group Performance Assessment

**Quarter 1 · Week 8 · Mon Sep 28, 2026 · One meeting · Paired · Environment: browser DevTools console**

The second Group PA of the year, and the one that collects on a promise the first
one made. Same shape as 1.6 — one block, in pairs, three thirds: **design, build,
demo**, with the design third first and neither partner opening an editor until the
written plan is done.

> **Update, 2026-10-26: 2.6.1 is a written plan, not a drawn chart.** The pair types a
> pseudocode plan into `README.md` (editor `text`, preview `console`) and the AI marks it
> against the problem they picked, three tries, best counts (`aiGrader`, summative,
> revisable, strict; four criteria, no points: `loop-walks-the-input`,
> `skip-or-stop-and-decide`, `classify-and-report`, `guards-bad-input`; passed when at
> least half are met or partly met). A read-only **Flow Chart** bar (`planChart`, a bus
> that fills up) shows the shape of a good plan for a problem that is not on the menu.
> Everything below about drawing, the hexagon and the diagram checks is how 2.6.1 used
> to work and is kept only where it still explains a decision; the live rules are in
> *What 2.6.1 checks now*. Part 2 reads the plan through `planFrom`.

## What Chapter 1's PA promised and this one delivers

1.6 charted a diamond that the program answered by *computing a boolean* and printing
it, because Chapter 1 had not taught a conditional. Its two branches were pseudocode
comments. Its own text said Chapter 2's Group PA is where they become code.

They become code here. Three things change, and nothing else about the shape does:

| | 1.6 (Sep 1) | 2.6 (Sep 28) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The diamond | computes a boolean, prints it; branches are comments | a real `if` / `else if` / `else` |
| The program's shape | a straight line | a loop with a decision inside it |
| Plan vocabulary | `START`, `INPUT`, `SET`, `IF`/`ELSE`, `PRINT`, `END` | + a loop line with its steps indented under it (§2.2), `CONTINUE`/`BREAK`, `SWITCH`/`CASE`, `THROW` |

## What it assesses

Everything in Chapter 2 at once. No section is assessed twice and none is left out:

| Section | Where it shows up |
| --- | --- |
| 2.1 Conditionals | The decision inside the loop — a real `if`/`else if`/`else`, with one `&&` or `\|\|` |
| 2.2 Algorithms and Loops | The loop that walks the input; the loop line with its repeating steps indented under it in the plan |
| 2.3 The switch Statement | The classification step — grouped cases, `break` on every one |
| 2.4 Loop Control and Nested Loops | The `break` or `continue` the brief forces, named in the demo — one each in Locker Sweep, Word Audit and Coin Drawer; Savings Run forces both |
| 2.5 Handling Errors with try/catch | A guard around the one input that can be bad, reporting `err.message` |

Chapter 1's five sections met the program in five different registers — one was a
naming convention, one was a paradigm the demo had to *name*. Chapter 2's five all
turn into executable lines. That is why the build is bigger than 1.6's at the same
25/50/25, and it is the pressure the timing below has to absorb.

## Timing for one 1.75-hour block

| Minutes | What |
| --- | --- |
| 0–8 | Pairs form, pick one problem from the menu of four, read the brief |
| 8–33 | **Design.** Talk it through, then type the plan in 2.6.1. Editors for Part 2 stay closed. |
| 33–75 | **Build.** 2.6.2. One screen, partners swap the keyboard at least once. |
| 75–95 | **Demo — Gallery Walk.** Two waves (8 hosts / 7 visitors). Each visitor sees 3 stations × ~3 min; teacher circulates with rubric and samples 4–5 live. |
| 95–105 | Slack. |

**The design third stays at 25 minutes and the build takes its two extra from the
pick-a-problem window**, which can afford them — the menu format is familiar by now
and the pairs are not new.

**The demo is a gallery walk, not a serial parade.** Two waves (8 hosts / 7 visitors) fit in 20 minutes with ~80 s per station; the teacher circulates with the rubric and samples 4–5 live demos. This is the first PA where programs are complex enough that peer review is valuable — 1.6's straight-line programs were too simple to benefit.

The design third does not get shortened, and the reason is worth stating because the
opposite looks true. §2.2's graded flowchart gate charts a decision, not a loop
(`2-2-12-a5-2-flowchart-decision`, whose own `sourceRef` records the divergence from the
plan's A2.2.0), so 2.6.1 is the first graded assessment that asks for a loop in a plan at
all. That is an argument for the full 25 minutes and for reading these plans closely, not
an argument that the shape is old news.

## Why the plan is 25% and still comes first

Same reason as 1.6, one turn harder. Two students who disagree about what happens
next have to settle it in the plan before either can type — and in Chapter 2 the
disagreement is no longer only *what* happens next but *how many times*. A pair that
cannot say where the loop ends in the plan will not discover it in code; they will
discover an infinite loop at minute 60.

A plan written after the code reads exactly like one, and the three tries and the
per-criterion feedback are there to catch a plan that only describes what the code did
after the fact.

## What this deliberately does not ask for

**No functions, no arrays, no objects.** Those are Chapter 3. The program is one
console script, top to bottom, with a loop in the middle.

**No functions in the plan.** Functions are Chapter 3; a plan that invents named functions
has over-sized a step, not discovered a need for decomposition. The `planChart` example
and the rubric both speak only of loops, `continue`/`break`, `switch` and `throw`.

**Iteration without arrays is the real constraint here**, and all four problems below
respect it. Chapter 2 can walk exactly two things: numbers generated by the loop
counter, and the characters of a string via `word[i]` and `word.length`
(`2-2-17a-example-walk-a-word`, with `.length` from 1.2.14). Any brief that needs a
list of values has to get them from a counter or a string. There is no third option
until §3.3.

## What 2.6.1 checks now

The old diagram rule block (eleven checks, `min-nodes: 8`, `min-process: 2`, the hexagon
that no check could require) is gone with the diagram. 2.6.1 now has four requirement
cards that mirror the four rubric items and a regex each; the regexes are only the
fallback when the AI is down (they are loose, they fail the untouched starter and pass
the reference). The AI is the judge:

| Rubric id | Credit when |
| --- | --- |
| `loop-walks-the-input` | a loop line walks or counts what the problem walks (lockers, characters, weeks, coins), its indented body does the problem's work, and the line after it is not indented |
| `skip-or-stop-and-decide` | the multiple-of-3 skip, the space skip, the no-deposit week and early stop, or the drawer-empty stop sits under its IF with `CONTINUE`/`BREAK` in the right role (swapped is wrong, not partial) |
| `classify-and-report` | after the loop, every outcome has its own condition (`SWITCH` or a chain of IFs) and the answer the problem asks for is reported before `END` |
| `guards-bad-input` | `n < 1`, an empty word or a negative target or amount is checked near the start, before the loop, with a `THROW` or stop |

Marking is lax on words and strict on logic, as everywhere in the course: any phrasing
and any keywords are accepted; reversed outcomes, an unreachable outcome, a comparison
that belongs after the loop sitting inside it, `CONTINUE`/`BREAK` swapped, a junk body
or another problem's words withhold the criteria that depend on them. Fixtures that hold
this line: `scripts/pa-plan-fixtures/2-6-1-ch2-group-pa-design-chart.json` (seven
reference plans, eight wrong ones), run with
`node scripts/test-pa-ai-graders.mjs --live --filter=2-6-1`.

## The menu

Four problems, matched in difficulty, all the same shape: **take one or two inputs,
walk something with a loop, decide inside the loop, classify one result with a
`switch`, guard the input that can be bad, report a running total and a verdict.**
Full statements are in 2.6.1.

1. **Locker Sweep** — walk locker numbers `1..n`, flip each on a rule, `continue`
   past the ones the rule skips, classify one locker's final state — open, closed, or
   never touched — with `switch`, guard a bad `n`
2. **Word Audit** — walk a phrase character by character, `continue` past the spaces,
   sort each remaining character into vowel / digit / other with `switch`, count all
   three, `throw` on an empty string
3. **Savings Run** — loop weeks, add a deposit, skip the weeks a rule excludes with
   `continue`, `break` the moment the target is hit, classify the outcome — hit early,
   hit on the last week, or missed — with `switch`, guard a negative target
4. **Coin Drawer** — a `while` loop making change from an amount, `switch` naming
   each denomination, `break` when the drawer runs dry, guard a negative amount

Pairs at adjacent tables should not pick the same one.

Every one of the four has a `break` or a `continue` in it that the brief forces
rather than suggests — §2.4 is the section a PA most easily assesses by accident,
and "there is a loop in it" is not evidence a pair understands loop *control*.

## The checklist's honest limits (measured 2026-09-18)

Three student lenses worked the built block. The advanced lens built a 15-line
program that satisfied all ten checks while solving nothing — a single `continue;
break;` pair and a two-case switch. Two fixes landed: r8 now demands **both** a
`break` and a `continue` in the loop, and r9 demands **three cases with breaks plus
a default** (matching the brief's own three-outcome wording). A token-matching
checklist verifies the presence of syntax, never the program's shape — a pair that
games it will produce a program that runs and says nothing true. The demo writeup
is the defense: Part 2's three test cases and Part 5's section-mapping are where a
paper program shows what it actually does. Read those before you read the code.

## Tries, feedback and the solution (from 2026-10-03)

Every part of this assessment gives each student **three tries**; the best one is the recorded score.
Partners are still graded separately, so each partner has their own three, even when they work
together. A banner at the top of every part says so before the first try and shows how many are left.

| Part | How a try is graded | After each try |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Plan | AI feedback on whether the typed pseudocode plan answers the chosen problem | per-criterion feedback and a score |
| Build | the visible checklist; **Grade** has the AI read the code and mark each card (the pattern check marks them if the AI is unavailable) | the checklist; Submit still needs it all green |
| Demo | AI, against the demo rubric | per-criterion feedback and a score |

**The build is the awkward one.** Its checklist is visible and Submit stays gated on every check
being green, so a student who gets there on the first submission has already got the best score
there is; the other two tries only matter if they edit and resubmit. It is capped for consistency, and
because the solution unlocks after the last try, a student who passed first time submits twice more
to see it. The alternative (reveal on any full-marks submission) was not taken, because the browser's
report is the only score the server has for a console part and would be a forgeable key to the solution.

**The solution.** After the last try the student sees the solution as plain-language pseudocode, once
the teacher releases it for the class (per part or per test, now or on a date, on the teacher page's
"Release solutions" panel). The text lives in `pa-pseudocode/<lessonId>.md`, which is git-ignored
because the repository is public (`pa-pseudocode/README.md`). Spec and record:
`.gauntlet/SPEC-attempt-caps.md`, `.gauntlet/attempt-caps-loop.json`.

## Teacher notes

- **Circulate during the design third, not the build third.** Unchanged from 1.6 and
  still the note that matters most.
- **A plan with no loop in it is the failure to look for.** The AI withholds
  `loop-walks-the-input` for it, but read the plans yourself: a pair can type a tidy
  straight-line plan that ignores the whole chapter, and the feedback only helps if they
  read it.
- **The one failure mode is still a silent partner.** The demo writeup asks who drove
  which third by name. Read that answer before you read the program.
- **A pair that finishes early has not finished — but ask the right question for
  their problem.** The zero-iteration input is the one to chase, because a loop that
  never enters is invisible where Chapter 1's straight line always ran. It is not the
  same question on all four:
  - **Savings Run** (`weeks = 0`) and **Coin Drawer** (`amount = 0`) have a genuine
    zero-iteration input their guard lets through — the guard rejects a *negative*
    target or amount, so zero is legal and the loop simply never runs. Ask for it.
  - **Locker Sweep** (`n = 0`) and **Word Audit** (the empty phrase) do **not**. Their
    zero-iteration input is precisely the input their guard throws on, so a pair that
    built the guard has already answered it. Ask them the boundary question instead:
    what happens at `n = 1`, or at a phrase that is nothing but spaces — which walks
    the loop and `continue`s past every character, reaching the report with all three
    counts at zero and no error thrown.
- **Hand this back as the study guide on Sep 28.** Every item type on the Ch 2 Test
  two days later has an analogue in the program the pair just built. Same policy as
  1.6 → 1.7, and it is not a secret.

- **Absent-partner policy:** the present member proceeds through all three thirds;
  the pair artifact and observed contribution are graded. The absent member completes
  an adjusted-scope solo make-up at a scheduled time; the present partner's grade is
  never affected by the make-up.

- **Odd-class trio policy:** one trio is permitted; roles (driver/navigator/recorder)
  rotate every 20 minutes; they produce one plan, one program, and the demo names
  all three and who drove which third. The 25/50/25 weights apply to the trio's
  single artifact.

## Still open

- **`switch (true)` is not taught, which is why every classification names three
  outcomes.** Verified: the string appears nowhere in `lessons/2-*`. §2.3 teaches
  `switch` on a value matched by strict equality, so a range test — "is the balance
  past the target" — cannot be a `switch` case at all; it has to be resolved into a
  value first, by `if`/`else if` or a ternary, and the `switch` then classifies that
  value. Two consequences the briefs above were rewritten to respect: a two-outcome
  classification is an `if`/`else` by §2.3.23's own teaching, so a brief asking for a
  `switch` over open/closed or hit/missed is asking for the thing the chapter tells
  students not to write; and Coin Drawer's `switch` is genuinely denomination-shaped
  (four cases plus a default) while Locker Sweep's and Savings Run's only become
  switch-shaped once the third outcome is named. **If 2.6.1 restates any of the four
  with two outcomes, it reintroduces this.**
- **§2.2's graded flowchart gate does not chart a loop.** Detailed under *Timing*
  above. `curriculum-plan.md` §2.2 specifies A2.2.0 as a hexagon/loop chart gating
  A2.2.1; the shipped `2-2-12-a5-2-flowchart-decision` charts a decision and says so
  in its own `sourceRef`. 2.6.1 no longer draws, so the loop-on-a-chart gap no longer
  bites here, but the mismatch with the plan's A2.2.0 text is still unresolved.
- **The loop-setup hexagon is no longer graded in 2.6.1.** The written equivalent is a
  loop line with the repeating steps indented under it and the exit after it. The
  read-only `planChart` still draws the bus's loop as a diamond that goes back until the
  stops run out; it is a reading aid, not a shape rule.
