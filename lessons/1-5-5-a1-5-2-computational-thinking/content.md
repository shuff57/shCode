## A1.5.2 — Computational Thinking

**What you'll practise:**
- Naming the four moves you already make when you solve a problem
- Telling them apart with a real example of each
- Saying what you deliberately ignored, and why that counts as a skill

Everything in the last two lessons — pseudocode, flowcharts, the convention — is a way of writing down thinking you were already doing. This assignment is about naming that thinking, because a move you can name is a move you can use on purpose.

### The four parts

| Part | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Decomposition** | Breaking a big problem into smaller pieces you can handle one at a time |
| **Pattern recognition** | Noticing where those pieces repeat, or look like something you have solved before |
| **Abstraction** | Ignoring the details that do not matter for the problem you are solving |
| **Algorithmic thinking** | Putting the pieces into a precise sequence someone else could follow |

They come in that order surprisingly often: break it up, notice what repeats, throw away what does not matter, write down the steps.

### What to write

About half a page, in two parts.

**Part 1 — Say what each one means,** in one or two sentences each, in your own words. Not the wording in the table above.

**Part 2 — Give one example of each,** all four drawn from **one task you actually did.** That can be code you have written this quarter, or an ordinary task — making a sandwich, getting to school, organising a group chat, planning a shift. Name the task, then walk through what the decomposition, the pattern, the abstraction and the algorithm were *in that specific case*.

### The one that trips people up

**Abstraction.** Most first attempts say something like "abstraction is making things simpler," which is true and tells the reader nothing.

Abstraction is *leaving something out on purpose*. So name what you left out and why it did not matter:

> *For the sandwich instructions it does not matter what brand the bread is, or whether the knife is metal or plastic. Those change nothing about the steps, so I left them out. What the robot does need is which side of the bread the jam goes on.*

That is an abstraction example. "I simplified it" is not. Your grade on that criterion depends on naming a specific detail you dropped.

### How this is graded

An AI grader reads it against five criteria and gives you written feedback. No points ride on it — you can revise and resubmit as many times as you want, and the feedback is the useful part.

It is looking for: all four parts explained, in your own words, one specific task named, all four illustrated from that task rather than re-explained, and an abstraction example that names something omitted.
